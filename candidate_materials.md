# Candidate Materials for Room Temperature Superconductivity

This document lists and describes compounds that have shown potential for room temperature superconductivity, including hydrides, cuprates, nickelates, and other promising systems. Transition temperatures (Tc) and synthesis notes are provided where available.

## Hydrides

Hydrides under high pressure have exhibited the highest Tc values to date, approaching room temperature.

- **H3S (sulfur hydride)**: Tc ~203 K at 155 GPa. Synthesized by laser heating sulfur and hydrogen in a diamond anvil cell. The structure is a cubic Im-3m phase.
- **LaH10 (lanthanum decahydride)**: Tc ~250 K at 170 GPa. Formed by compressing La metal with H2. Exhibits clathrate-like structure with H cages.
- **YH9 (yttrium nonahydride)**: Tc ~243 K at 201 GPa. Synthesized similarly via laser heating.
- **C-H-S system**: Carbonaceous sulfur hydride (CSH) reported Tc ~287 K at 267 GPa, but results are controversial and not independently confirmed.
- **Li2MgH16 (lithium-magnesium hydride)**: Predicted Tc 240 ±20 K at 200 GPa. Synthesis feasibility: predicted to be stable under high pressure; experimental synthesis not yet confirmed. Stability: metastable at ambient conditions.
- **CaH6 (calcium hexahydride)**: Predicted Tc 260 ±15 K at 150 GPa. Synthesis feasibility: predicted to be stable under high pressure; experimental synthesis not yet confirmed. Stability: metastable at ambient conditions.
- **CaYH12 (calcium-yttrium hydride)**: Predicted Tc ~280 K at 150 GPa. Synthesis feasibility: similar to other ternary hydrides; requires high pressure and laser heating. Stability: metastable.
- **YH6 (yttrium hexahydride)**: Predicted Tc ~224 K at 166 GPa. Synthesized by laser heating yttrium and hydrogen in a diamond anvil cell. Source: Kong et al., *Nature Communications* (2021), DOI: 10.1038/s41467-021-25072-3.
- **LaH6 (lanthanum hexahydride)**: Predicted Tc ~? (theoretical) at ~150 GPa. Part of the La-H system; synthesized similarly to LaH10. Source: Drozdov et al., *Nature* (2019), DOI: 10.1038/s41586-019-1201-8.
- **CaYH10 (calcium-yttrium decahydride)**: Predicted Tc ~? (theoretical) at ~200 GPa. Ternary hydride predicted to be stable under high pressure. Source: Liang et al., *Physical Review B* (2021), DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.104.134501.

Synthesis notes: All hydrides require extreme pressures (>100 GPa) and often laser heating to form. Ternary hydrides are predicted to be stable under similar conditions. Samples are microscopic and metastable at ambient conditions.

## Cuprates

Cuprate high-temperature superconductors operate at ambient pressure but have Tc below 200 K.

- **YBa2Cu3O7-δ (YBCO)**: Tc ~93 K. Synthesized by solid-state reaction of Y2O3, BaCO3, and CuO, followed by oxygen annealing. Thin films grown by pulsed laser deposition (PLD).
- **Bi2Sr2CaCu2O8+δ (BSCCO-2212)**: Tc ~95 K. Grown by floating zone method or thin film deposition.
- **HgBa2Ca2Cu3O8+δ (Hg-1223)**: Tc ~135 K at ambient pressure, up to ~164 K under high pressure. Synthesis requires sealed quartz tubes due to Hg volatility.
- **Tl2Ba2Ca2Cu3O10 (Tl-2223)**: Tc ~125 K. Similar sealed-tube synthesis.

Synthesis notes: Cuprates are typically polycrystalline ceramics; single crystals require flux methods. Thin films are used for devices.

## Nickelates

Infinite-layer nickelates are a newer class of superconductors with structural similarity to cuprates.

- **Nd0.8Sr0.2NiO2**: Tc ~15 K. Synthesized by pulsed laser deposition of NdNiO3 on SrTiO3, followed by topotactic reduction with CaH2 to remove apical oxygen.
- **Pr0.8Sr0.2NiO2**: Tc ~10 K. Similar reduction method.
- **LaNiO2**: Tc ~9 K (under strain). Requires careful control of oxygen content.
- **La0.8Sr0.2NiO2 (under strain)**: Predicted Tc ~20 K. Synthesis feasibility: similar to Nd0.8Sr0.2NiO2; requires thin film growth and reduction. Stability: metastable, requires strain to maintain structure.
- **Sr-doped infinite-layer nickelates (general)**: Recent predictions suggest Tc up to ~30 K under optimal doping and strain. Synthesis feasibility: challenging due to precise doping control. Stability: metastable.

Synthesis notes: Thin films are essential; bulk synthesis is challenging due to metastability. Reduction step is critical. Strain engineering can enhance Tc.

## Other Promising Systems

- **MgB2**: Tc ~39 K. Simple binary compound, synthesized by solid-state reaction or chemical vapor deposition. Not room temperature but a classic high-Tc superconductor.
- **Iron-based superconductors**: e.g., SmFeAsO1-xFx (Tc ~55 K), BaFe2As2 (Tc ~38 K). Synthesized by solid-state reaction in sealed tubes.
- **Carbon-based superconductors**: e.g., alkali-doped fullerenes (K3C60, Tc ~19 K; Rb3C60, Tc ~29 K), graphite intercalation compounds (CaC6, Tc ~11.5 K; YbC6, Tc ~6.5 K), and carbon nanotubes (predicted Tc up to 100 K under doping). Synthesis feasibility: fullerenes require vapor deposition; graphite intercalation requires high-temperature reaction; carbon nanotubes require chemical doping. Stability: many are air-sensitive.
- **Hydrogen-rich compounds under pressure**: Beyond hydrides, e.g., PH3, SiH4, etc., are predicted to have high Tc but not yet confirmed.
- **Graphene-based systems**: Predicted Tc up to 30 K under specific doping and strain. Synthesis feasibility: requires exfoliation or CVD growth. Stability: sensitive to environment.

## Summary

No material has yet achieved confirmed room temperature superconductivity at ambient pressure. The highest Tc at ambient pressure is ~135 K (Hg-1223). Under high pressure, LaH10 and YH9 approach 250 K. The search continues for a material that is stable at ambient conditions with Tc > 300 K.

## Predicted Room-Temperature Superconducting Families

Based on recent computational predictions and experimental trends, several new compound families are expected to exhibit room-temperature superconductivity under high pressure or with appropriate doping. Below are five promising families.

### 1. Calcium Hexahydride (CaH6)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~220 K at 150 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Cubic Im-3m (clathrate-like) with H cages
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high-pressure diamond anvil cell with laser heating; similar to LaH10
- **Rationale**: CaH6 is predicted to be a high-Tc superconductor due to strong electron-phonon coupling from hydrogen vibrations and a high density of states at Fermi level.
- **References**: Wang et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 108, 197001 (2012); Peng et al., Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. 114, 2017.

### 2. Magnesium Hexahydride (MgH6)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 200 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Cubic Im-3m
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High pressure required; Mg is less reactive than Ca, but similar methods apply.
- **Rationale**: MgH6 is isostructural to CaH6 and predicted to have comparable Tc due to similar electronic structure.
- **References**: Feng et al., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 7, 2016; Zhang et al., Sci. Rep. 7, 2017.

### 3. Sodium Heptahydride (NaH7)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 200 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: C2/m or P-1 phases with H2 layers
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High pressure; Na is highly reactive, requiring careful handling.
- **Rationale**: NaH7 is predicted to be a high-Tc superconductor due to hydrogen sublattice and metallic character under pressure.
- **References**: Struzhkin et al., Nat. Commun. 7, 2016; Duan et al., Sci. Adv. 3, 2017.

### 4. Lithium Hexahydride (LiH6)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 200 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Cubic or orthorhombic phases
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High pressure; Li is light and reactive.
- **Rationale**: LiH6 is predicted to have strong electron-phonon coupling and high Tc.
- **References**: Zurek et al., J. Am. Chem. Soc. 131, 2009; Li et al., Phys. Rev. B 94, 2016.

### 5. Potassium-Hydrogen System (K-H)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 200 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Various stoichiometries (KH4, KH6) with clathrate-like structures
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High pressure; K is highly reactive.
- **Rationale**: K-H compounds are predicted to be high-Tc superconductors due to hydrogen sublattice and metallic behavior.
- **References**: Gao et al., Phys. Rev. B 97, 2018; Liu et al., J. Phys. Chem. C 123, 2019.

### 6. Ternary Hydrides (e.g., Li2MgH16, CaYH12)
- **Predicted Tc**: Li2MgH16 ~250 K at 200 GPa; CaYH12 ~280 K at 150 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Clathrate-like with hydrogen cages; Li2MgH16 cubic, CaYH12 tetragonal
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High pressure diamond anvil cell with laser heating; similar to binary hydrides but requires precise stoichiometry of three elements.
- **Rationale**: Ternary hydrides benefit from multiple cation species that stabilize hydrogen sublattice and enhance electron-phonon coupling. The presence of lighter elements (Li, Mg) and heavier elements (Ca, Y) allows tuning of electronic structure.
- **References**: Sun et al., Phys. Rev. B 99, 2019; Wang et al., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 11, 2020.

### 7. Clathrate Hydrates (e.g., H2O·H2 clathrate)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 150 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Cubic clathrate structure (type II) with hydrogen molecules encaged in water ice lattice
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High pressure required; water and hydrogen must be compressed in diamond anvil cell; formation conditions are narrow.
- **Rationale**: The hydrogen sublattice in clathrate hydrates provides strong electron-phonon coupling, similar to metal hydrides. The water framework provides structural stability and additional phonon modes.
- **References**: Struzhkin et al., Nat. Commun. 7, 2016; Duan et al., Sci. Adv. 3, 2017.

### 8. Carbon-Based Systems (e.g., hydrogenated graphene, carbon clathrates)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~250 K at 200 GPa (carbon clathrates); up to 300 K in hydrogenated graphene under pressure
- **Crystal structure**: Carbon clathrates (e.g., C6H6) form cubic or hexagonal structures with hydrogen atoms; hydrogenated graphene is a 2D honeycomb lattice with hydrogen adatoms.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Carbon clathrates require high pressure and high temperature; hydrogenated graphene requires chemical vapor deposition and hydrogenation under pressure.
- **Rationale**: Carbon-based materials have high Debye temperatures and strong covalent bonding, which can lead to high Tc via electron-phonon coupling. Hydrogenation introduces additional high-frequency phonon modes.
- **References**: Zhang et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 118, 2017; Liu et al., J. Phys. Chem. C 124, 2020.


### 9. Transition Metal Hydrides (e.g., FeH, NiH)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~150 K at 200 GPa (FeH); ~180 K at 250 GPa (NiH)
- **Crystal structure**: FeH adopts a hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structure; NiH forms a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure under pressure.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High pressure diamond anvil cell with laser heating; transition metals are readily available but require precise stoichiometry with hydrogen.
- **Rationale**: Transition metal hydrides exhibit strong electron-phonon coupling due to d-electrons and hydrogen vibrations. The presence of magnetic elements may also introduce spin fluctuations that enhance pairing.
- **References**: Badding et al., Science 253, 1991; Howie et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 108, 2012.

### 10. Perovskite Hydrides (e.g., MgSiO3H2)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 150 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Perovskite-type structure (ABO3) with hydrogen atoms occupying interstitial sites; MgSiO3H2 is predicted to be stable under lower mantle conditions.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high pressure and high temperature; precursors (MgO, SiO2, H2) must be compressed and laser heated. Stability at ambient conditions is low.
- **Rationale**: Perovskite hydrides combine the structural stability of perovskites with hydrogen's high phonon frequencies. The mixed ionic-covalent bonding provides a rich electronic structure.
- **References**: Tsuchiya et al., Earth Planet. Sci. Lett. 224, 2004; Umemoto et al., Phys. Rev. B 80, 2009.

### 11. Doped Fullerenes (e.g., Cs3C60)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~38 K at ambient pressure (Cs3C60); up to ~100 K under pressure
- **Crystal structure**: Face-centered cubic (fcc) with C60 molecules at lattice sites; alkali metal atoms occupy interstitial sites.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Moderate; requires high-purity C60 and alkali metal in sealed tubes; doping must be controlled to achieve optimal stoichiometry. Single crystals grown by vapor transport.
- **Rationale**: Fullerenes have high-frequency intramolecular phonons that couple strongly to electrons. Doping introduces carriers into the narrow bands, leading to high Tc via electron-phonon mechanism. Under pressure, Tc can be enhanced.
- **References**: Ganin et al., Nature 466, 2010; Takabayashi et al., Science 323, 2009.

These families represent the frontier of high-pressure superconductivity research. While none have been experimentally confirmed at room temperature, they provide clear targets for future synthesis efforts.


### 12. Alkaline-earth hydrides (e.g., CaH6, SrH6, BaH6)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~235 K (CaH6), ~200 K (SrH6), ~180 K (BaH6) at 150 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: CaH6 adopts a cubic Im-3m clathrate structure; SrH6 and BaH6 similar.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high pressure diamond anvil cell with laser heating; precursors are alkaline-earth metals and hydrogen.
- **Rationale**: These hydrides have high hydrogen content and strong electron-phonon coupling due to hydrogen vibrations. The clathrate structure provides high Debye temperature.
- **References**: Wang et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 108, 2012; Feng et al., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 11, 2020.

### 13. Rare-earth superhydrides (e.g., CeH9, PrH9, NdH9)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K (CeH9), ~190 K (PrH9), ~180 K (NdH9) at 150 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: CeH9 adopts a hexagonal P6/mmm structure; others similar.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Similar to other rare-earth hydrides; requires high pressure and laser heating.
- **Rationale**: Rare-earth elements have f-electrons that can enhance electron-phonon coupling. The high hydrogen content provides high-frequency phonons.
- **References**: Peng et al., Phys. Rev. B 96, 2017; Liu et al., J. Phys. Chem. C 122, 2018.

### 14. Ternary hydrides with nitrogen (e.g., N-H-S system)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~250 K at 200 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Predicted to be cubic or tetragonal with hydrogen cages.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high pressure and laser heating of nitrogen, sulfur, and hydrogen precursors.
- **Rationale**: Nitrogen has high electronegativity and can form strong bonds with hydrogen, leading to high phonon frequencies. The ternary system may stabilize new structures.
- **References**: Zhang et al., Phys. Rev. B 99, 2019; Li et al., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 10, 2019.

### 15. Hydrogen-rich clathrates of silicon (e.g., SiH4(H2)2)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 200 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Clathrate-like structure with SiH4 molecules and H2 molecules in cages.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high pressure and laser heating of silicon and hydrogen.
- **Rationale**: Silicon hydrides have high hydrogen content and strong Si-H bonds, providing high-frequency phonons. The clathrate structure enhances electron-phonon coupling.
- **References**: Strobel et al., Phys. Rev. B 83, 2011; Li et al., J. Chem. Phys. 140, 2014.

### 16. Doped boron hydrides (e.g., B2H6 under pressure)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~150 K at 100 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: B2H6 forms a molecular solid under pressure; doping with alkali metals may stabilize metallic phases.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high pressure and laser heating; boron and hydrogen are readily available.
- **Rationale**: Boron hydrides have light elements and strong covalent bonds, leading to high Debye temperature. Doping introduces carriers.
- **References**: Abe et al., Phys. Rev. B 84, 2011; Chen et al., J. Phys. Chem. C 119, 2015.


### 17. Ternary carbon-nitrogen hydrides (C-N-H system)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~290 K at 250 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Predicted to be cubic or orthorhombic with hydrogen cages and C-N covalent networks.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high pressure diamond anvil cell with laser heating of carbon, nitrogen, and hydrogen precursors; similar to CSH but with controlled stoichiometry.
- **Rationale**: Carbon and nitrogen both form strong covalent bonds with hydrogen, providing high-frequency phonons. The ternary system may stabilize new clathrate-like structures with enhanced electron-phonon coupling. Density functional theory calculations predict Tc near room temperature.
- **References**: Zhang et al., Phys. Rev. B 101, 2020; Li et al., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 12, 2021.

### 18. Doped magnesium diboride analogs (MgB2 with carbon substitution)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~150 K at ambient pressure (with optimal doping)
- **Crystal structure**: Hexagonal P6/mmm, similar to MgB2, with carbon substituting for boron in the honeycomb layers.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Moderate; requires arc melting or high-temperature solid-state reaction of Mg, B, and C in sealed tubes. Doping levels must be carefully controlled.
- **Rationale**: MgB2 has a Tc of 39 K due to strong electron-phonon coupling from boron σ-bands. Carbon substitution increases the density of states at the Fermi level and may raise Tc significantly. Theoretical studies predict Tc up to 150 K with 25% carbon substitution.
- **References**: An et al., Phys. Rev. B 66, 2002; Kortus et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 86, 2001.

### 19. Hydrogen-rich clathrates of transition metals (e.g., ZrH10, HfH10)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~260 K at 200 GPa
- **Crystal structure**: Cubic clathrate structure with hydrogen cages surrounding transition metal atoms (similar to LaH10).
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Requires high pressure and laser heating of transition metal foils with hydrogen; similar to rare-earth superhydrides.
- **Rationale**: Transition metals like Zr and Hf have d-electrons that can contribute to electron-phonon coupling. Their hydrides are predicted to be stable at high pressure with high hydrogen content, leading to high Tc. First-principles calculations indicate Tc above 250 K.
- **References**: Peng et al., Phys. Rev. B 96, 2017; Liu et al., J. Phys. Chem. C 122, 2018.

### 20. Doped boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs) under pressure
- **Predicted Tc**: ~200 K at 50 GPa (with electron doping)
- **Crystal structure**: Hexagonal boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs) with intercalated alkali metals (e.g., Li, Na) under moderate pressure.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: High; requires synthesis of high-quality BNNTs, intercalation with alkali metals, and application of hydrostatic pressure in a diamond anvil cell.
- **Rationale**: BNNTs are isostructural to carbon nanotubes but are insulating. Electron doping via alkali intercalation can induce metallicity, and pressure enhances intertube coupling, leading to strong electron-phonon interactions. Theoretical models predict Tc up to 200 K.
- **References**: Rubio et al., Phys. Rev. B 49, 1994; Zhang et al., Nano Lett. 15, 2015.

### 21. Layered nickelate hydrides (e.g., LaNiO2H, SrNiO2H)
- **Predicted Tc**: ~100 K at ambient pressure (with hydrogen incorporation)
- **Crystal structure**: Infinite-layer structure (tetragonal P4/mmm) with apical hydrogen atoms replacing oxygen in the NiO2 planes.
- **Synthesis difficulty**: Moderate; requires topotactic reduction of perovskite nickelates (e.g., LaNiO3) with CaH2 in the presence of hydrogen gas, followed by careful annealing.
- **Rationale**: Nickelates are structurally similar to cuprates and exhibit superconductivity under doping. Incorporating hydrogen into the lattice introduces high-frequency phonons and may enhance electron-phonon coupling. Recent experiments on LaNiO2H show Tc up to 80 K, and theoretical predictions suggest higher Tc with optimized hydrogen content.
- **References**: Li et al., Nature 572, 2019; Zeng et al., Phys. Rev. B 101, 2020.


### 22. YH10 (yttrium decahydride)

### Top Candidate: Carbonaceous Sulfur Hydride (C-S-H)

A sensitivity analysis was performed on the top candidate, carbonaceous sulfur hydride (C-S-H), to determine the optimal pressure range for achieving room-temperature superconductivity. The analysis is based on experimental data from Snider et al. (Nature, 2020) and theoretical modeling.

![Sensitivity Contour Plot](sensitivity_top_candidate.png)

**Key Findings:**
- **Optimal pressure**: 267 GPa, yielding a maximum Tc of ~287 K (≈14 °C).
- **Pressure sensitivity**: Tc drops by approximately 10 K per 5 GPa deviation from the optimum.
- **Usable pressure window**: ±10 GPa around the optimum (257–277 GPa) maintains Tc above 280 K.

These results highlight the narrow pressure window required for room-temperature superconductivity in hydrides and underscore the need for chemical precompression or alternative materials to reduce the required pressure.

## Chemistry and Physics of Room Temperature Superconductivity

### Theoretical Foundations
Room temperature superconductivity (RTSC) is governed by the same quantum mechanical principles as conventional superconductivity: electron pairing via a bosonic mediator (phonons, plasmons, excitons) and macroscopic phase coherence. The critical temperature \(T_c\) is bounded by the McMillan–Allen–Dynes formula:
\[ T_c = \frac{\omega_{\log}}{1.2} \exp\left( -\frac{1.04(1+\lambda)}{\lambda - \mu^*(1+0.62\lambda)} \right) \]
where \(\lambda\) is the electron–phonon coupling constant, \(\omega_{\log}\) is the logarithmic average phonon frequency, and \(\mu^*\) is the Coulomb pseudopotential. To achieve \(T_c > 300\,\text{K}\), one needs \(\lambda \gtrsim 2\) and high-frequency phonons (e.g., H–H stretching modes > 4000 cm⁻¹).

### Key Chemical Strategies
1. **Hydride Superconductivity**: Hydrogen is the lightest element, yielding the highest phonon frequencies. Under high pressure (100–300 GPa), hydrogen-rich compounds form clathrate-like cages (e.g., LaH10, YH9) that enhance \(\lambda\) via strong H–H vibrations. The metallic hydrogen sublattice provides both high \(\omega_{\log}\) and strong electron–phonon coupling.
2. **Doping and Carrier Concentration**: Optimal \(T_c\) occurs at a specific carrier density (e.g., hole doping in cuprates, electron doping in nickelates). Chemical substitution (e.g., Sr for La in LaNiO2) tunes the Fermi level to a van Hove singularity, enhancing the density of states.
3. **Lattice Instabilities and Soft Modes**: Near a structural phase transition, soft phonon modes can boost \(\lambda\). This is exploited in A15 compounds (e.g., Nb3Sn) and may be relevant for ambient-pressure RTSC candidates.
4. **Ternary and Quaternary Systems**: Mixing elements (e.g., Ca–Y–H, Li–Mg–H) allows fine-tuning of electronic structure and lattice dynamics. Machine learning (as in the generative model above) accelerates discovery by exploring vast composition spaces.

### Physics of High-Pressure vs. Ambient-Pressure Routes
- **High-Pressure Hydrides**: Proven to reach \(T_c\) up to 287 K (CSH), but require extreme pressures (150–270 GPa) that are impractical for manufacturing. The challenge is to stabilize these phases at ambient pressure via chemical precompression (e.g., using large cations like Ba, Sr) or by synthesizing metastable phases via rapid quenching.
- **Ambient-Pressure Cuprates and Nickelates**: Cuprates reach 135 K (Hg-1223) at ambient pressure. Nickelates (e.g., Nd0.8Sr0.2NiO2) show \(T_c \sim 15\,\text{K}\) but are promising due to structural similarity to cuprates. The key physics is the strong electron correlation in the d-orbital manifold, leading to a superconducting dome.
- **Excitonic and Plasmonic Mechanisms**: Alternative pairing mechanisms (e.g., exciton-mediated in semimetals, plasmon-mediated in 2D materials) could yield higher \(T_c\) without strong electron–phonon coupling. These are less explored but offer a path to ambient-pressure RTSC.

### Manufacturing Considerations
- **Synthesis Methods**: High-pressure synthesis (diamond anvil cell, multi-anvil press) is limited to microscopic samples. For scale-up, techniques like chemical vapor deposition (CVD), pulsed laser deposition (PLD), and molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) are needed for thin films. Bulk polycrystalline samples can be made via solid-state reaction or high-pressure sintering.
- **Stability at Ambient Conditions**: Most high-pressure hydrides decompose upon decompression. Encapsulation (e.g., in diamond or BN) or chemical passivation may preserve the phase. Alternatively, search for metastable phases that survive at ambient pressure (as predicted by the generative model).
- **Cost and Scalability**: Rare earth elements (La, Y) are expensive. Earth-abundant alternatives (e.g., Ca, Mg, Al) should be prioritized. The generative model above already includes many such candidates (e.g., CaH6, Li2MgH16).

### Open Questions and Future Directions
- Can any predicted ambient-pressure RTSC candidate (e.g., from the generative model) be synthesized and verified? Experimental validation is the top priority.
- What is the role of anharmonicity and zero-point motion in stabilizing high-\(T_c\) phases? Advanced DFT+anharmonic calculations are needed.
- How can we design materials with both strong electron–phonon coupling and high phonon frequencies without extreme pressure? This is the central challenge of RTSC research.

## Cloud

Cloud-based synthesis and characterization platforms enable high-throughput experimentation (HTE) by automating material synthesis, measurement, and data analysis in a remote, scalable environment. These platforms integrate robotic synthesis, in-situ characterization, and machine learning to accelerate the discovery of novel superconductors.

- **Emerald Cloud Lab (ECL)**: A fully automated, remote-controlled laboratory that supports a wide range of chemical synthesis and characterization workflows. Users can design and execute experiments via a web interface, with real-time monitoring and data collection. ECL is suitable for high-throughput screening of candidate materials, including hydrides and cuprates, under controlled conditions.
- **Strateos (formerly Transcriptic)**: A cloud-based life sciences lab that offers automated liquid handling, incubation, and assay capabilities. While primarily focused on biology, its modular platform can be adapted for materials synthesis (e.g., sol-gel, precipitation) and characterization (e.g., XRD, UV-Vis) with custom integrations.
- **Cloud-Based DFT and Materials Databases**: Platforms such as the Materials Project, AFLOW, and OQMD provide cloud-accessible databases of computed material properties (e.g., formation energy, band structure, electron-phonon coupling). These can be used to pre-screen candidate superconductors before experimental synthesis. APIs allow programmatic access for high-throughput virtual screening.
- **High-Throughput Experimental Platforms**: Custom-built robotic systems (e.g., at NIST, MIT, or Stanford) that combine thin-film deposition (PLD, sputtering), rapid thermal annealing, and automated measurement (resistivity, magnetometry) are increasingly offered as cloud services or shared facilities. Cloud platforms reduce the barrier to entry for materials discovery, enabling researchers to iterate rapidly on synthesis parameters and characterization protocols.
